Aerial Survey / Capture Standard

Flying for Orthomosaics

An orthomosaic is only as good as the flight that fed it. Three settings decide almost everything: where the camera points, the path you fly, and how much consecutive photos overlap.

A Reconstruction recovers 3D by seeing the same ground point from many angles. Every parameter below exists to serve that one requirement. Software cannot add coverage that the flight did not capture — a badly flown site produces a bad map in every tool, commercial or open source.

The target here is visual-grade: relative geometry sound, appearance finished, no stated centimetre accuracy. Survey-grade work adds Ground Control Points on top of this same flight discipline — it does not replace it.

Setting 01

Nadir — camera straight down

Nadir means the gimbal points down and stays there. We fly at −80°, ten degrees off vertical, locked. Not forward, not drifting. You are not filming the site; you are scanning it. The slight tilt is deliberate: a perfectly vertical block lets error accumulate into a dome, and a few degrees off vertical breaks that up.

The reason is scale. With the camera pointing straight down, every part of the frame sits at roughly the same distance from the lens, so a metre of ground occupies the same number of pixels wherever it falls in the image. Tilt the camera and the far edge of the frame is much further away than the near edge — the same angular slice covers more and more ground as it recedes. That is a photograph, not a map, and you cannot measure across it.

Setting 02

The grid — parallel passes, and overshoot the edges

Fly the site in parallel straight lines, back and forth, the way you would mow a lawn. Constant altitude, constant speed, camera locked down throughout.

The detail people miss is the margin. Photos at the boundary of your grid only have neighbours on one side, so they reconstruct poorly and drag the edge of your map down with them. Extend the grid at least one full pass beyond the area you actually care about, and fly the turns outside the site rather than over it.

Setting 03

Overlap — the one that actually decides quality

Overlap is how much of the ground two consecutive photos share, and it comes in two numbers:

Forward overlap (75–85%) runs along a single pass — each photo shares roughly four-fifths of its view with the one before it. Side overlap (65–75%) runs between neighbouring passes, which is why the passes sit so close together.

Eighty percent sounds absurdly wasteful. It is not. The goal is that every point on the site appears in five to nine photographs from different positions — that redundancy is the depth information. Cut overlap and you do not get a slightly worse map; you get holes, warped surfaces, and stitching seams that no software setting recovers.

Failure modes

Three things that ruin a mission

01

Changing light

Fly under flat overcast, or with the sun high. Cloud shadows moving across a twenty-minute mission produce a patchwork orthomosaic, and no amount of colour grading unpicks it afterwards.

02

Stopping at the boundary

Edge photos have neighbours on one side only. The reconstruction thins out exactly where your client is looking — the property line. Overshoot by a full pass.

03

Re-planning after a battery swap

A grid mission outlasts one battery, which is fine. Resume the same mission. Re-planning mid-site shifts the grid and leaves a gap along the join. Budget on roughly 25–30 minutes of real flying per battery, not the rated figure.

04

Steel on site

Rebar, structural steel and reinforced slabs distort the compass and degrade the GPS this whole workflow already treats as marginal. Calibrate away from the structure, watch for heading drift on the first pass, and treat position data from directly above heavy steel as suspect.

05

Wind, on a sub-250g airframe

The aircraft is rated to roughly 12 m/s and is more wind-sensitive than a purpose-built mapping platform. Wind pushes it off the planned line and forces attitude changes that tilt the camera off nadir — which is the one thing the whole capture depends on.

Beyond the basic grid

When a plain nadir grid isn't enough

Vertical structures. A pure nadir grid sees roofs and almost nothing of walls. Buildings and anything tall need either a second grid flown perpendicular to the first (a cross-hatch, which doubles the viewing angles on every surface) or an orbit with the gimbal tilted to about −45°, flown as a separate pass and reconstructed together.

Sloped sites. Constant altitude above the takeoff point means constant altitude above sea level, not above ground. On a slope the effective altitude — and therefore the resolution and the real overlap — drift across the site. Either fly terrain-following if the planner supports it, or step the mission into altitude bands.

Rolling shutter. The aircraft almost certainly reads its sensor out line by line rather than exposing the whole frame at once, so movement during readout skews the image. Flying slowly is the mitigation, and it is another reason speed is a capture parameter rather than a convenience.

Before committing a full mission, take a few test shots at working altitude and confirm on the controller that exposure is sane, the horizon is level, the gimbal is genuinely at −80°, and GPS is being written.

Execution

Two ways to fly it

Do not hand-fly a grid. Consumer DJI drones have no native mapping-grid planner, so either import a computed mission or place waypoints yourself.

How we do it

A mission generated for this aircraft

The boundary is drawn once, and the mission is generated from it: pass spacing from the real sensor and the target overlap, photo positions computed, the camera angle written in, and the file laid out exactly as DJI Fly lays out its own.

  • Overlap is calculated, and then measured on the ground before the file is loaded
  • Repeatable — the same boundary regenerates the same waypoints months later
  • Extends a full pass beyond the boundary automatically
  • Splits into battery-sized parts when a site exceeds DJI's 200-waypoint limit

This is not a supported import path

There is no import button in DJI Fly. The file is written into DJI Fly's own waypoint folder on the controller, over a mission created there by hand — a file-replacement route, not a feature. What that means in practice:

  • The controller must be one DJI Fly can write for. On an iPhone there is no way in at all.
  • The mission does take the photos. A mission built in DJI Fly carries a photo action at each waypoint, so capture is part of the flight plan rather than a timer you start. Our generated missions carry the same actions. Confirm on the first pass regardless.
  • A DJI Fly update can break it silently by changing the folder path or naming. Check after every update, before a job rather than during one.

Verify capture is actually firing on the first pass of every mission. A flown grid with no photos is the most expensive possible outcome.

Pre-flight checklist

  • Forward 80% / side 70% entered in the planner
