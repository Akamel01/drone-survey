## 2. Pilot certification and registration (CARs Part IX, 2026)

**Certificate needed: Pilot Certificate – Advanced Operations.** Basic Operations is legally sufficient only if every flight stays more than 30 m horizontally from all bystanders, in uncontrolled airspace, more than 5.6 km from a certified airport, and more than 1.9 km from a heliport ([Basic operations](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/drone-operation-categories-pilot-certificates/basic-operations)). Construction-site surveys routinely fail at least one of these (crews and passersby within 30 m, and many urban/suburban sites sit inside Class C/D/E controlled airspace), so Advanced is the realistic requirement.

**What distinguishes Basic from Advanced**, per Transport Canada's own operation-category pages:

- Basic: drone ≤25 kg, VLOS, >30 m from any person, uncontrolled airspace only, >5.6 km from a certified airport, >1.9 km from a heliport. No RPAS Safety Assurance requirement on the drone. ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/drone-operation-categories-pilot-certificates/basic-operations))
- Advanced: with the certificate **and** a drone that carries the matching manufacturer Safety Assurance Declaration (SAD), you can fly in controlled airspace with NAV CANADA/DND permission, closer to airports/heliports, between 5–30 m of bystanders (CAR 901.69(b), Standard 922.05), within 5 m of or over bystanders (CAR 901.69(c), Standard 922.06 — separate declaration required), sheltered operations around structures, extended-VLOS with a visual observer, and medium drones (25–150 kg) VLOS. ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/drone-operation-categories-pilot-certificates/advanced-operations))

**Exams, flight review, fees** (all from Transport Canada's own fee tables, pages dated 2026-03-19):

- Small Basic exam: 35 multiple-choice questions, 90 minutes, 65% pass mark, minimum age 14, fee **$10.17 CAD**, issuance of the Basic certificate is included in that fee and is immediate. ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/drone-operation-categories-pilot-certificates/basic-operations))
- Small Advanced exam: 50 multiple-choice questions, 60 minutes, 80% pass mark, minimum age 16, fee **$10.17 CAD**. A separate, in-person **flight review** with a Transport Canada flight reviewer is then mandatory. Certificate issuance fee: **$25.43 CAD**, issued immediately after payment. Both exams and the certificate application go through the Drone Management Portal. ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/drone-operation-categories-pilot-certificates/advanced-operations))
- Transport Canada does not publish a fixed flight-review fee because reviewers are independent; third-party estimates cluster around $250–400 CAD, but I could not confirm this figure against a Transport Canada source (see "could not verify").
- Neither certificate expires, but a **24-month recency requirement** applies: retake the relevant exam, complete another flight review, or complete recurrent training (including a Transport Canada–endorsed self-paced study program for 2026–27), and keep the record for 24 months. ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/drone-pilot-licensing/drone-pilot-study-resources/recency-requirements-self-paced-study-program))
- All fees are indexed annually on April 1 under the *Service Fees Act*; the figures above are current as of the March 2026 page update. ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/drone-zone/drone-zone-issue-7-may-2026))

**Registration at 252–253 g:** yes, required. "All drones weighing at least 250 g must be registered." Fee is **$10.17 CAD**, a one-time issuance (no renewal), done in about 3 minutes through the Drone Management Portal with a credit card, Apple Pay, or Google Pay. The registration number must be **clearly marked on the aircraft** using permanent marker, permanent label, or engraving; Transport Canada also recommends adding your name and contact information. Fines for flying unregistered/unmarked reach $5,000 (individual) or $25,000 (corporation). Foreign pilots cannot register a drone and need an SFOC-RPAS instead. ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/registering-your-drone))

**If the aircraft were genuinely under 250 g**, it would be a microdrone: no registration, no pilot certificate needed, and the RPAS Safety Assurance requirement disappears. You would still have to fly non-recklessly, stay clear of emergency sites and restricted airspace, get an SFOC to fly at an "advertised event," and respect trespass and privacy law. Transport Canada explicitly places the burden of confirming actual operating weight on the pilot, and its own civil aviation inspectors have said the ±4 g manufacturing tolerance on the Mini 5 Pro is not being treated as a major issue provided you fly on standard OEM batteries — but Transport Canada's head of the RPAS unit has also pushed back on any claim of "automatic" treatment as a microdrone. Given your reported real-world weight of 252–253 g, this report treats the aircraft as **not** a microdrone throughout, per your instruction. ([Transport Canada microdrone rules](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/drone-operation-categories-pilot-certificates/microdrones); [DroneXL reporting on TC's statement](https://dronexl.co/2025/09/25/transport-canada-clears-dji-mini-5-pro/))

## 3. What the 2025 rule changes altered

The overhaul rolled out in two phases ([Transport Canada's own 2025 summary](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/2025-summary-changes-canada-drone-regulations)):

- **April 1, 2025**: registration and exam fees increased (registration rose from $5 to roughly $10); the new Level 1 Complex exam and RPAS Operator Certificate (RPOC) application pathway opened; SFOC fees were introduced for later implementation.
- **November 4, 2025**: the bulk of the substantive changes took effect —
  - A new lower-risk **BVLOS** certification pathway (Level 1 Complex: age 18+, pass the L1C exam, 20 hours of ground school, a flight review, and an RPOC with documented operator policies/procedures).
  - Existing **Advanced** certificate holders automatically gained three new privileges without reapplying: **sheltered operations** (flying around a structure within 30 m vertically / 61 m horizontally of it, without maintaining direct VLOS or a visual observer, subject to the drone's Standard 922.04 declaration), **extended visual line-of-sight (EVLOS)** using a trained visual observer within 3.7 km, and VLOS operation of **medium drones** (25–150 kg) where the drone carries the matching safety declaration.
  - A new **medium RPAS** category (25–150 kg) that no longer automatically requires an SFOC-RPAS for VLOS operations, provided the drone is declared safe for the specific operation.
  - Flying a **microdrone at an advertised event** (concert, festival, outdoor public event) now requires an SFOC, closing a gap that previously let sub-250 g drones fly at such events with no paperwork.
  - A new fee schedule for SFOC-RPAS applications took effect: roughly $20 (very low complexity), $75 (low), $900 (medium), $2,000 (high). ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/2025-summary-changes-canada-drone-regulations))
  - A new 24-month pilot recency requirement was introduced across Basic, Advanced, and Level 1 Complex certificates.

**What actually matters for this operator:** the Advanced-certificate expansion (sheltered operations and EVLOS are genuinely useful for orbiting a structure for 3D reconstruction without a visual observer walking the full perimeter) and the fee increases. BVLOS, Level 1 Complex, RPOC, and the medium-RPAS category are not relevant to a solo VLOS operator flying a sub-25 kg aircraft.

## 5. Insurance

**Not legally mandatory** for Basic or Advanced operations. Transport Canada's own guidance states it plainly: "Insurance is not required for standard operation categories but is recommended. Proof of insurance may be required for operations that need a Special Flight Operations Certificate (SFOC-RPAS)." ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/flying-your-drone-safely-legally))

**Commercially, it is close to mandatory.** Construction general contractors and site owners routinely require proof of commercial general liability / aviation liability before granting site access, and $1,000,000 CAD is the most commonly requested minimum; some ask for $2,000,000. Providers serving small Canadian operators include SkyWatch.AI (Canadian plans reported around $446–466 CAD/year including hull coverage), Avion Insurance (~$500/year for liability + hull), and general commercial brokers (Zensurance, Westland/"Drone Insurance Canada," and others) who place drone risks with aviation underwriters. These figures come from insurer/broker marketing pages, not a government source, and will vary with your claims history, coverage limits, and drone value — treat them as ballpark, not quoted rates. ([SkyWatch](https://www.skywatch.ai/ca/annual-drone-insurance-plan), [general market overview](https://rpaswilco.com/blog/drone-insurance-canada))

## 6. Airspace and site access

**Federal aviation rules (apply regardless of who owns the land):**

- Maximum altitude: 400 ft (122 m) AGL, or 100 ft (30 m) above a building/structure if flying within 200 ft (61 m) of it horizontally — this is also exactly the altitude/distance envelope Transport Canada uses to define "sheltered operations." (CAR 901.25; corroborated by [Transport Canada's sheltered-operations description](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/drone-operation-categories-pilot-certificates/advanced-operations))
- Distance from bystanders: Basic >30 m; Advanced 5–30 m (with 922.05 declaration, which your drone has) or <5 m/overhead (with 922.06, which your drone does **not** have).
- Distance from aerodromes: >5.6 km (3 NM) from a certified airport and >1.9 km (1 NM) from a heliport under Basic; closer is possible under Advanced with NAV CANADA (or DND, for military aerodromes) permission. ([source](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/drone-operation-categories-pilot-certificates/advanced-operations))
- Controlled airspace (Class C/D/E — common around urban cores and near any towered airport) requires an Advanced certificate, a drone declared for 922.04 (yours qualifies), and a NAV CANADA **RPAS Flight Authorization** obtained through the **NAV Drone** app (web or mobile). You plan the operation (location, geometry, max height, category, activity, timing), the system validates it as Allowed, Authorization required, or Not allowed; if authorization is required you submit a Permission Request, which is automatically approved when the requested altitude is under the grid cell's threshold, or manually reviewed by NAV CANADA otherwise. A downloadable RPAS Operation Summary serves as your proof of authorization. ([NAV CANADA's own NAV Drone documentation](https://www.navcanada.ca/en/nav-drone-web-operation-planning-and-permission-requests.aspx))
- All drones are aircraft under the Aeronautics Act and are prohibited from restricted airspace without separate permission, regardless of weight class.

**Property/trespass — a separate body of law, not CARs.** Drones fly in national airspace, not "your property's airspace." Canadian case law (following the English *Bernstein of Leigh v. Skyviews & General Ltd.*) holds that a landowner's rights extend only to the height reasonably necessary for ordinary use and enjoyment of the land — a drone passing overhead briefly is unlikely to be trespass, but launching, landing, or loitering/hovering over someone's land without permission is a materially higher-risk act. Transport Canada's own rules explicitly say pilots "must respect trespassing laws" and "must respect the privacy rights of others" — the CARs themselves are not concerned with privacy or trespass, so compliance with CARs does not protect you from a civil trespass or privacy claim. ([legal commentary on this point](https://www.mltaikins.com/insights/can-private-property-owners-ban-drone-overflights/); [Transport Canada](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/flying-your-drone-safely-legally))

**Practical implication for a survey business:** get written permission for the takeoff/landing site from the property owner or general contractor, and separately think through whether your flight path or image footprint will cross adjoining private parcels (very likely for full orthomosaic coverage of a site boundary) — that overflight is a civil-law question the client's own site-access agreement will not automatically cover.

## 7. Privacy — the important part

**Which regime applies.** PIPEDA is the federal default and always applies to interprovincial or international handling of personal information in the course of commercial activity. British Columbia's PIPA, Alberta's PIPA, and Quebec's *Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector* (as amended by Law 25) are each designated "substantially similar" to PIPEDA and **displace** PIPEDA for commercial activity that occurs wholly within that province. Practically: if you operate only within BC, Alberta, or Quebec, that province's law — not PIPEDA — governs your local activity; if you're based in Ontario or any other province without its own private-sector law, or your activity crosses provincial/international lines (e.g., cloud storage outside the province, a client relationship spanning provinces), PIPEDA applies. There is no small-business carve-out in any of these regimes, including Law 25. ([OPC on which law applies where](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/r_o_p/prov-pipeda/))

**Does aerial imagery of a construction site count as personal information?** Yes, potentially. The governing test (from Federal Court jurisprudence that the OPC applies) is whether there is a "serious possibility that an individual could be identified," alone or in combination with other information — a face, a gait, a license plate, or a recognizable vehicle's presence pattern over time can all qualify, even if no name is attached. A single frame of a distant, blurry figure may not be personal information; a set of images that lets you track the same vehicle or person's presence across multiple site visits is more likely to be. ([OPC's general drone research note](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/research/explore-privacy-research/2013/drones_201303/) — note this specific OPC publication dates to 2013 and predates PIPEDA's current enforcement posture and the 2025 aviation changes; treat it as background on the identifiability concept, not as current operational guidance)

**Does resolution/altitude matter, and is Canadian guidance explicit about it?** There is no numeric resolution or altitude threshold in PIPEDA itself, and I found no OPC guidance for private-sector operators that sets one. The one concrete figure available comes from a **federal government internal** source: Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat **Privacy Implementation Notice 2025-03** (effective July 21, 2025), which tells *government institutions* — not private businesses, and it is issued under the Privacy Act, not PIPEDA — to consider using a larger Ground Sample Distance, "such as 25–30 cm per pixel," as one way to reduce identifiability when limiting collection is a goal, alongside automated blurring of faces/plates and prompt deletion of inadvertently captured personal information. ([source](https://www.canada.ca/en/treasury-board-secretariat/services/access-information-privacy/access-information-privacy-notices/privacy-implementation-notice-2025-03.html))

At your planned mapping altitude of 60–100 m with a ground sample distance of a few centimetres per pixel, that resolution is **far finer** than the 25–30 cm/pixel the federal notice treats as identity-reducing — meaning faces are unlikely to be resolvable at that altitude in a straight-down (nadir) pass, but license plates and vehicle identification are plausible at a few cm/pixel depending on angle and sensor, and any oblique or close-orbit passes you fly for 3D-reconstruction texture capture (which typically requires much lower, closer, angled shots around structures than the nadir mapping pass) meaningfully raise identifiability risk relative to the nadir survey. Repeated visits to the same site compound this, since pattern-of-presence tracking is exactly the scenario Canadian guidance flags as more likely to constitute personal information. **Do not assume your imagery is automatically non-personal because of altitude** — document your own identifiability assessment per project rather than relying on a general assumption.

**Obligations — consent, notification, retention, deletion:**

- *Consent*: PIPEDA and the provincial equivalents require "informed, free and meaningful" consent proportional to sensitivity, calibrated to what a reasonable person would expect. Getting individual consent from every construction worker or passerby a drone might incidentally capture is not realistic; the more defensible approach is (a) limiting collection to what the mapping purpose actually requires, (b) notifying people generally — site entrance signage, a toolbox-talk mention to the crew, a clause in your contract with the site owner/GC — and (c) relying on the "reasonable purpose" framing in PIPEDA (and equivalents) for incidental, non-targeted capture, while treating any deliberate identification of a specific individual as requiring their consent. Your contract with the property owner is **not** consent from their workers or the public — it only covers your relationship with the client. ([CBA guidance for commercial drone operators](https://cba.org/Sections/Air-and-Space-Law/Member-Articles/Privacy-laws-and-drones-Legal-considerations-and-best-practices-for-commercial-drone-operators))
- *Retention*: PIPEDA's Principle 4.5 requires that personal information "no longer required to fulfil the identified purposes" be destroyed, erased, or anonymized. The Act sets **no specific numeric retention period** — the OPC's own guidance is that retention must be "reasonable and necessary" for the stated purpose, backed by a documented retention schedule and destruction procedure. ([OPC, PIPEDA Principle 5](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/p_principle/principles/p_use/); [OPC retention/disposal guidance](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/business-privacy/breaches-and-safeguards/safeguarding-personal-information/gd_rd_201406/))
- *Notification and deletion-on-request*: under PIPEDA-family law, individuals generally have a right to know what personal information about them an organization holds and, in some circumstances, to request its correction or withdraw consent for its continued use — build a simple process for handling such a request even if you expect it rarely (a worker asking to be blurred out of a public-facing marketing image, for example).

**Is a 12-month retention limit defensible?** Yes, in principle — there is no minimum or maximum retention period fixed by any of these statutes, so 12 months is neither too short nor too long as a matter of law. What makes it defensible is not the number itself but what accompanies it:

1. A **written retention policy** stating the number and the purpose it serves (e.g., matching a typical construction-dispute or warranty window, or your own professional-liability/insurance claims period). If you cannot articulate why 12 months rather than 3 or 36, a regulator or a client's lawyer can reasonably ask.
2. A **documented purpose** for the original collection, communicated to the client and, where practicable, to site personnel.
3. **Deletion procedures that are actually executed** at the 12-month mark — an unenforced policy is a bigger compliance risk than an honestly-longer retention period that is followed.
4. If any Quebec client or site is involved: Law 25 layers on additional obligations regardless of your company size — a designated person responsible for personal information protection (by default, the person with highest authority in the enterprise, i.e., you as sole proprietor, unless you delegate), a published privacy policy, mandatory breach notification, and a Privacy Impact Assessment requirement for projects involving the acquisition or overhaul of an information system handling personal information (unlikely to be triggered by a simple drone survey workflow, but worth a one-time check per project). Law 25's headline penalties — administrative monetary penalties up to the greater of $10 million or 2% of worldwide turnover, and penal fines up to the greater of $25 million or 4% of worldwide turnover — are wildly disproportionate to a solo operator's realistic risk profile, but they signal that Quebec's regulator (the CAI) has real teeth, and the baseline paperwork (policy, breach process, retention schedule) costs little to have in place. ([Law 25 penalty structure](https://www.osler.com/en/insights/blogs/risk/law-25-a-new-enforcement-scheme-for-protection-of-personal-information-in-the-private-sector-in-que/))

**Raw imagery vs. derived products (orthomosaic/3D model) — a distinction worth making, but not one any regulator has spelled out for drones specifically.** Raw frames are where the privacy risk concentrates: individual images can contain a recognizable face, a legible plate, or an identifiable person in a specific place and time. A processed orthomosaic or 3D mesh is a different artifact — photogrammetry pipelines typically blend, decimate, and texture-average many overlapping frames, and moving objects (people, vehicles) are often ghosted, blurred, or dropped entirely by multi-image consensus filtering, which tends to reduce identifiability in the deliverable relative to the source frames. A reasonable, risk-based policy is to delete or aggressively time-limit **raw** imagery while retaining the **derived deliverable** — the actual client product — for a longer project-record period, provided you have checked that the specific output does not itself resolve an identifiable individual. I want to be explicit that this raw-vs-derived treatment is my own inference from applying PIPEDA's purpose-limitation and minimization principles to how photogrammetry actually works, not a rule stated by the OPC, a provincial commissioner, or Transport Canada — treat it as a defensible practice, not confirmed law.

## Biggest risks

1. **The 5 m "over-people" gap in the Mini 5 Pro's declaration.** Your drone is cleared for controlled airspace and for 5–30 m from bystanders, but not for flying within 5 m of, or over, anyone not part of the operation. Active construction sites have workers moving unpredictably; this is probably the single most likely way this business runs into a CARs violation, and it doesn't require bad luck — it requires an ordinary moment where someone walks under the flight path.
2. **Weight variance.** DJI's stated ±4 g manufacturing tolerance plus battery choice means the aircraft's actual weight could shift. You've already decided to treat it as ≥250 g, which is the right conservative call given your reported 252–253 g — but if you ever fly a different unit or battery, re-weigh it. Transport Canada has been explicit that this determination is the pilot's responsibility, not the manufacturer's marketing number.
3. **Declarations can be silently invalidated.** Transport Canada's own list documentation states declarations may be invalidated after being listed, at which point your drone's registration certificate is reissued to reflect the change. A model that qualifies today is not guaranteed to qualify next quarter — build a periodic re-check into your operating procedure, not just a one-time lookup before your first job.
4. **Controlled airspace near urban construction is common, not exceptional.** Many downtown and near-airport sites sit inside Class C/D/E airspace. A missed NAV Drone authorization is a CARs violation on its own, and depending on your insurer's policy wording, an unauthorized flight could also void liability coverage for anything that goes wrong on that flight — worth confirming directly with your insurer.
5. **Repeat visits to the same site raise privacy risk over time**, because tracking the same person's or vehicle's presence pattern across visits is exactly the scenario Canadian privacy guidance treats as more likely to constitute personal information, even where a single visit's imagery would not.
6. **Overflight of adjoining properties.** Full-coverage orthomosaic and 3D capture commonly requires flight paths and image footprints that extend beyond the client site's boundary onto neighbouring private land — a civil trespass/nuisance exposure that your contract with the client does not resolve.
7. **Quebec exposure is disproportionate on paper.** If you ever take a Quebec client or fly a Quebec site, Law 25's obligations apply with no small-business exemption, and its penalty ceilings are large relative to a one-person operation — realistic enforcement risk against a sole proprietor is low, but the baseline compliance paperwork is cheap enough that skipping it isn't worth the tail risk.
8. **Insurance is not legally required but is functionally required.** Because it's not a CARs mandate, it's easy to deprioritize — but without it you likely cannot get contracted for real construction work at all, and you carry uninsured liability for property damage or injury from every flight.

## What I could not verify

- **NAV CANADA's fee (if any) for a standard small-drone Advanced VLOS controlled-airspace authorization.** NAV CANADA's own NAV Drone documentation describes the workflow without stating a per-request fee, which suggests it may currently be free for standard requests, but I could not find this stated as a definite $0 on a primary NAV CANADA fee-schedule page. Third-party blogs cite $15–35 per authorization; I could not confirm that figure against navcanada.ca's own service-charges pages, and it may be conflated with the discontinued third-party RPAS WILCO service.
- **The exact market price of a Transport Canada–recognized Advanced flight review.** This is set by independent reviewers, not Transport Canada, so there's no government figure to cite; the commonly quoted $250–400 CAD range comes from training-provider and blog sources only.
- **Whether NAV Drone/controlled-airspace authorization formally applies to genuine sub-250 g microdrones.** Transport Canada's microdrone rules page does not explicitly address controlled airspace, and secondary sources disagree in practice. This is moot for your aircraft as instructed (treated as ≥250 g throughout), but flagged for completeness since the ±4 g tolerance question keeps surfacing.
- **BC and Alberta drone-specific privacy guidance.** Unlike Quebec's Law 25 commentary or the general PIPEDA materials, I found no current, drone-specific published position from the Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner for BC or Alberta — only general "privacy management program" joint guidance and an older joint fact sheet reference on street-level imaging technology. Provincial nuances for BC/Alberta beyond general PIPA text are inferred by analogy to PIPEDA reasoning, not confirmed by a BC/Alberta regulator statement specifically about drones.
- **The raw-vs-derived-product retention distinction** (shorter retention for raw frames, longer for a processed orthomosaic/3D model) is my own reasoned application of PIPEDA's minimization and purpose-limitation principles to how photogrammetry pipelines actually behave — I could not find a Canadian regulator decision or guidance document that states this distinction for drone survey deliverables specifically.
- **Whether incidental (non-biometric-matching) drone video of individuals counts as "sensitive personal information" under Quebec's Law 25.** Law 25 treats biometric identification/verification data as sensitive; ordinary photographic capture without biometric matching is likely just standard personal information, but I found no Commission d'accès à l'information (CAI) ruling addressing drone photography specifically on this point.
- **Currency of the DJI Mini 5 Pro's Safety Assurance Declaration status by the time you read this.** I queried Transport Canada's live table directly during this research (page dated 2026-06-26); Transport Canada explicitly warns declarations can be invalidated after listing, so re-verify at [the live list](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/drone-safety/learn-rules-you-fly-your-drone/choosing-right-drone-advanced-complex-operations) before relying on it for an actual paid job.
