# Deep Verification Pass — Orthomosaic Engine, Compute Topology, and the Wider Field

Prepared September 2026. This pass had two jobs: (A) resolve five specific claims the prior research passes left unverified or under-sourced, and (B) widen the search past the two owned machines to rented compute, managed processing, engines not yet considered, and non-RTK accuracy tricks. Every claim below carries a source URL; where a primary source could not be reached, that is stated explicitly rather than papered over.

---

## What changed

**CONFIRMED, with a sharper reason than before:**
- Stay on OpenDroneMap (ODM) as the orthomosaic engine. The prior pass recommended this on operational grounds (RealityScan's Linux support is experimental). This pass adds a **licensing** reason that is arguably more serious: RealityScan's EULA explicitly bars using the software "on a … service bureau or similar basis" (§A1), and the classic, well-established meaning of "service bureau" — a company that uses its own equipment/software to process other parties' work for a fee (the same term used for payroll bureaus and 3D-printing bureaus) — describes a solo drone-survey business almost exactly. This is genuinely ambiguous (see below) and was **not resolved** even with direct access to Epic's own documentation, because the primary EULA text is login-walled. Given ODM already does the job with no such restriction, there is no reason to accept this risk.
- RealityScan's headless-authentication problem on Linux is **real, not a single unverified report**. An Epic staff member (JakubVanko) confirms directly, in an official Epic Developer Community forum thread, that "RealityScan requires a display manager even in headless mode" and provides a workaround (a virtual X display via `pyvirtualdisplay`, or pointing `DISPLAY` at an existing Xorg session). This is now first-party-confirmed, not a rumor (§A2).
- ODM's known weak points (thin structures, water, vegetation, default-parameter softness) are unchanged from prior research; nothing new surfaced that alters that picture.

**OVERTURNED or materially corrected:**
- **A4 is corrected downward.** The prior pass's "confirmed OOM at ~1,100 4K images" was read as a 12GB-VRAM data point. It was not: the GitHub issue in question (nerfstudio #3214) reports the failure on a GPU with **21.67GB total capacity**, not 12GB. On a 12GB card the real ceiling is meaningfully lower than 1,100 images — order of a few hundred, not "just over a thousand." This changes the risk assessment for splat training on the owned RTX 4070 SUPER: it is tighter than previously stated.
- **A2's kernel requirement is softer than the prior wording implied.** Epic's Linux documentation, fetched directly this pass, states Ubuntu 24.04 (kernel 6.14) / Fedora 39 (kernel 6.10) as the **recommended** configuration, not explicitly flagged as a hard-blocking minimum. It should still be treated as load-bearing (untested below it), but "recommended" and "minimum" are not the same claim, and the prior report's phrasing overstated certainty here.
- **The "two owned machines are the whole world" framing is wrong, and this is the single biggest addition this pass makes.** Rented GPU compute is cheap enough (see §B1) that it changes several earlier conclusions: it removes the 12GB VRAM ceiling for splat training (rent a 24GB+ card for the hour), it removes GPU/RAM contention with the production stack as a structural risk (offload the heavy job instead of negotiating for local resources), and — critically — it does **not** rescue RealityScan, because rented Windows GPU instances exist cheaply (TensorDock, ~$0.35–0.37/hr for an RTX 4090 with native Windows 10), but the EULA's service-bureau ambiguity is platform-independent. Renting a Windows box solves the *operational* objection to RealityScan and does nothing about the *licensing* objection.

**STILL UNRESOLVED:**
- Whether RealityScan's free tier legally permits this exact business model (solo operator, own drone, own compute, sells the deliverable) is not resolved by this pass either. See §A1 and the "still unverified" section — this needed the primary EULA text or a direct answer from Epic and got neither.
- A precise, vendor-quoted RAM/disk table for ODM at 200/500/1,000 images remains unavailable; ODM does not publish one. This pass adds real numbers from the community and one MDPI paper (blocked from full text — see below) but they are still practitioner reports, not a spec sheet.

**Bottom-line recommendation, restated with what's new:**
- **Orthomosaic engine: OpenDroneMap.** Unchanged, now for two independent reasons (operational + licensing risk on the alternative) instead of one.
- **Compute topology: stop treating the shared Ubuntu box as the only option for heavy jobs.** For a solo operator doing ~8 jobs/month, renting a GPU instance per job (RunPod, TensorDock, Vast.ai) costs on the order of a few dollars per job, has zero or near-zero egress fees on the providers checked, and removes both the RAM-contention risk with the production stack and the 12GB VRAM ceiling on splat training. This does not replace the local box — day-to-day inspection, the Runner, and light jobs still belong there — but it is a real, cheap release valve for anything that would otherwise force a hard tradeoff against the production stack, and it deserves a line in the design document it currently does not have.

## Part A — Resolving the specific ambiguities

### A1. RealityScan/RealityCapture licence: the decisive question

**What is confirmed, from Epic/Capturing Reality's own current pages and corroborating secondary sources:**
- The free-under-$1M tier is still in force in 2026, unchanged in structure since Epic's 2021 acquisition, and reconfirmed as recently as the RealityScan 2.0/2.1/2.2 release cycle (November 2025–June 2026). [RealityScan license page](https://www.realityscan.com/license) · [CG Channel: RealityScan 2.2](https://www.cgchannel.com/2026/06/epic-games-releases-realityscan-2-2-with-amd-gpu-support/)
- The threshold is **gross revenue**, not funding raised: "During any period that you, together with any entities in your corporate group, have generated less than $1,000,000 USD in gross revenue over the last 12 months, the Seat subscription requirement will not apply." Above it, seats are $1,250/user/year standalone or $1,850/user/year bundled with Unreal Engine + Twinmotion.
- Separately, the EULA defines "non-commercial purpose" as "a purpose without any direct or indirect commercial benefit," and states "all uses of the Software by or on behalf of commercial entities are regarded as being for commercial purposes." This confirms the free tier is **not** restricted to non-commercial use — commercial use is the entire point of the revenue-threshold framework — so ordinary commercial photogrammetry work is contemplated by the licence in principle.
- **The decisive, unresolved catch:** the EULA separately states the software may not be made available to third parties "on a software-as-a-service, hosted service, time-sharing, service bureau or similar basis." I could not reach the primary EULA text directly — `realityscan.com/en-US/eula` and `realityscan.com/license` both redirect through an Epic Games account login wall that this session's tools cannot pass, and `unrealengine.com/en-US/eula/realityscan` returned HTTP 403. This clause's exact wording is corroborated identically across two independent search-engine text extractions of the same page, which is reasonable (if not primary-source) evidence the quote is accurate, but I still have not read it in its full surrounding context (definitions, carve-outs, examples) myself.

**Why this matters and why it is genuinely ambiguous, not just cautious hedging:** "Service bureau" has two live meanings in commercial practice, and they point in opposite directions for this business:
1. **Narrow/IT reading** (the one the clause's own neighboring terms suggest — "software-as-a-service, hosted service, time-sharing"): don't let third parties log into or operate *your copy of the software* remotely, i.e., don't resell RealityScan's own functionality as a hosted service. Under this reading, a solo operator who personally flies a drone, personally runs RealityScan on their own machine, and delivers a finished orthomosaic/mesh to a paying client is just an ordinary commercial software user — no different from an architecture firm selling drawings made with a single-seat CAD licence. [Generic EULA "service bureau" clause explanation](https://www.termsfeed.com/blog/eula-saas-apps/)
2. **Classic/industry reading**: a "service bureau" is, historically and currently, *a company that uses its own equipment or software to process other parties' work for a fee* — the term used for payroll service bureaus, print/reprographics service bureaus, and 3D-printing service bureaus (a well-established modern usage: "service bureaus are a significant sector within the … 3D printing industry that allow customers to [outsource] production" rather than buy their own printer). [Wikipedia: Service bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_bureau) · [Law Insider: Service Bureau Processing definition](https://www.lawinsider.com/dictionary/service-bureau-processing) Under *this* reading, "we own RealityScan and process drone imagery you give us into a mapping deliverable, for a fee" is arguably the textbook definition of a photogrammetry service bureau, and would be squarely inside the prohibition regardless of revenue.

I found no primary-source clarification resolving which reading Epic intends, no case law or enforcement precedent, and no direct statement from Epic addressing drone-survey businesses specifically. I did find market evidence that at least some drone-service companies openly advertise using RealityCapture/RealityScan for client work (e.g., a Southeast Florida photogrammetry business), which is circumstantial evidence Epic does not aggressively enforce the broad reading in practice — but "not enforced against small operators so far" is not the same as "permitted," and is not something to build a business's legal footing on. [Drone Services Texas: photogrammetry software review](https://droneservicestx.com/photogrammetry-software-review-commercial-work/)

**Practical conclusion:** this ambiguity does not change the recommendation (ODM was already the right choice on operational grounds), but it does close off "put RealityScan on a rented Windows box and use it for paid client jobs" as a casual workaround. If RealityScan is ever wanted for real client work — not just a personal/non-commercial side-by-side quality check — get written confirmation from Epic's own licensing contact before invoicing a client for anything it touched.

### A2. RealityScan on Linux

- **Kernel requirement:** Epic's own Linux documentation lists "Ubuntu 24.04 (Kernel 6.14) or Fedora 39 (Kernel 6.10), 64-bit" under system requirements — phrased as the supported configuration, not explicitly marked "hard minimum, will refuse to run below this." [Epic dev docs: RealityScan for Linux](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/realityscan/realityscan-for-linux?lang=en-US) Treat it as load-bearing regardless: nothing found in this pass describes anyone successfully running it below that kernel.
- **Does Ubuntu 24.04's HWE stack satisfy it?** Yes, as of the 24.04.3 point release (2025-08-07), Ubuntu's Hardware Enablement kernel stack backported Linux 6.14 from the 25.04 release onto 24.04 LTS, installable via `linux-generic-hwe-24.04`; 24.04.4 (early 2026) moved the HWE kernel further, to 6.17. [Ubuntu Discourse: Kernel HWE update for Noble 24.04.3](https://discourse.ubuntu.com/t/kernel-hwe-update-for-the-upcoming-noble-24-04-3-point-release/62259) · [Phoronix: Ubuntu 24.04.4 LTS with Linux 6.17 HWE kernel](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Ubuntu-24.04.4-LTS) The host in this design currently runs 6.8.0-134-generic — the GA kernel, not HWE — so satisfying RealityScan's stated requirement means an actual kernel upgrade and reboot, not a no-op.
- **How disruptive is that, concretely?** I found no source specific to this exact host's stack, so this is reasoned engineering judgment, flagged as such: an HWE kernel bump on Ubuntu is a routine, well-trodden operation (`apt install --install-recommends linux-generic-hwe-24.04`, reboot), and NVIDIA's proprietary driver is normally packaged with DKMS, which rebuilds its kernel module automatically against the new kernel on the next boot. The real cost is not technical difficulty, it is a **mandatory reboot** — every one of the ~40 production containers goes down for the reboot window, however brief, and the exercise should be scheduled and validated (confirm the NVIDIA driver actually reloads, confirm `nvidia-smi` and the container runtime both come back) rather than assumed. This is a maintenance-window decision, not a blocker.
- **Containerized?** Yes, confirmed directly from Epic's own docs: "RealityScan Linux can run fully inside a Docker container with GPU passthrough," explicitly positioned for "cloud and large-scale automated environments," with "Docker with the NVIDIA Container Toolkit" named as "the recommended and supported solution" for virtualized GPU access (traditional virtualization — WSL2, VirtualBox, VMware — is explicitly called out as typically unable to expose full GPU capability). [Epic dev docs: Known Issues and Limitations (Linux)](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/realityscan/known-issues-and-limitations-linux)
- **Is the headless-auth hang reproduced by anyone else, and has Epic shipped anything less experimental?** Yes to the first, confirmed first-party this pass (a correction of the prior report's "single unverified secondary source" framing). An Epic Developer Community forum thread ("RealityScan - Fully Headless Linux") has a user (NecroticNanite) reporting RealityScan working over RDP but failing silently over SSH with no display, and Epic staff member **JakubVanko confirms directly**: "RealityScan requires a display manager even in headless mode," and walks through the fix — either a virtual display via Python's `pyvirtualdisplay` (works, adds ~45 seconds startup overhead) or pointing the `DISPLAY` environment variable at an already-running Xorg session (`DISPLAY=:1 realityscan-cli -stdConsole [options]`), located via `ps aux | grep Xorg` or `ls /tmp/.X11-unix/`. The same thread also surfaces two more real, previously undocumented Linux issues: `-stdConsole` not returning output over SSH, and a requirement for Windows-style paths (`Y:/`) rather than native Linux paths in some contexts — plus an explicit admission from Epic that "alignment results are non-deterministic across platforms." [Epic Developer Community forum: RealityScan - Fully Headless Linux](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/realityscan-fully-headless-linux/2682217) On "less experimental since": nothing found in this pass suggests the "experimental" label has been lifted as of the 2.2 release (June 2026); the fixes above are workarounds, not an upstream resolution.

### A3. ODM resource requirements — a real table, not an interpolation

No single authoritative ODM-published table exists (confirmed again this pass — the official install docs still don't carry one), but concrete, sourced data points now replace the prior pass's interpolation:

| Images (≈48MP-class datasets) | RAM | Disk (intermediate) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 250 | ~16GB | — | Community-cited scaling reference point |
| 500 | ~32GB | 20–40GB at `pc-quality high` | Same source; disk figure independently corroborated in a separate community thread |
| 1,000 | ~64GB | — | "seems to be sufficient" per community synthesis; a 20-core CPU is cited as roughly optimal, 10 cores only "slightly" slower |
| ~4,000–4,600 | >256GB without split-merge | — | A real forum case: a 4,577-image/5472×3468px job on a 64GB machine ran out of RAM; the ODM maintainer's diagnosis was **not** "you need more RAM" but "you misused `--split`" — `--split 400` sets the *submodel starting size*, not the final submodel size including overlap, so actual submodels were far larger than 400 images and blew the RAM budget. Lowering `--split` (to 200 or less) was the fix, not more hardware. |
| ~8,000 | up to 256GB | — | MDPI performance-analysis paper's cited figure (see caveat below) |

Sources: [OpenDroneMap Community: RAM requirements and cloud based processing](https://community.opendronemap.org/t/ram-requirements-and-cloud-based-processing/7199) · [OpenDroneMap Community: ODM keeps running out of RAM](https://community.opendronemap.org/t/odm-keeps-running-out-of-ram/14674) · [OpenDroneMap: Large Datasets docs](https://docs.opendronemap.org/large/) · [MDPI: OpenDroneMap Multi-Platform Performance Analysis](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-7086/3/3/23) (abstract/secondary citations only — the full paper returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch both this pass and the prior one; treat its 8,000-image/256GB figure as secondary-sourced, not independently confirmed from the primary text)

**What `--feature-quality`, `--pc-quality`, and split-merge actually do to these numbers:**
- `--pc-quality ultra` combined with `--feature-quality ultra` on a 500-image dataset was reported to run **3–4 days** wall-clock — this is the practical cost of chasing the artifact-reduction gains the prior report documented (straighter edges, less roof waviness). [ODM Community discussion on feature-quality/pc-quality](https://community.opendronemap.org/t/question-regarding-feature-quality-and-pc-quality/6038)
- Split-merge (`--split N --split-overlap Xm`) genuinely reduces peak RAM by processing the dataset in chunks (submodels), each roughly `N` images plus enough surrounding overlap to merge cleanly — the documented starting point is `--split 400 --split-overlap 100`. The RAM-blowup case above is the sharpest warning available: **the split size does not mean what it looks like it means**, and a naive `--split 400` on a dense, high-overlap dataset can produce submodels well over 400 images, defeating the entire point. [OpenDroneMap: Large Datasets docs](https://docs.opendronemap.org/large/)

**Would 62GB RAM (shared with a production stack) and 77GB free disk complete a 500-image job?** Plausibly, but with real qualification, not a clean yes. 32GB is the community reference point for 500 images at default settings — the 62GB host clears that with room to spare *if the production stack's own footprint is known and subtracted*, which the design document itself flags as unmeasured. Disk is the tighter constraint: 500 raw 48MP images at typical JPEG sizes alone can run 7–12GB, and `pc-quality high` intermediate data adds another 20–40GB on top of that — on a 77GB free volume shared with a live production stack's own churn, a single 500-image/high-quality run can plausibly consume most or all of the free disk, with no margin for anything else running concurrently.

**Realistic maximum dataset for this host, as currently configured (before the planned dedicated SSD):** roughly **300–500 images at default-to-high quality settings**, and disk — not RAM — is the binding constraint. This is consistent with, not a contradiction of, the design document's own stated risk ("no client-facing quality report... measure before committing to dataset sizes"); this pass adds the specific number and names disk, not RAM, as the actual limiter given the host's current disk headroom.

### A4. splatfacto on 12GB VRAM — corrected ceiling

The prior pass's headline number needs a direct correction: the confirmed OOM case (nerfstudio GitHub issue #3214: 1,116 4K images, nerfstudio 1.1.2 + gsplat 1.0.0) reports the GPU's **total capacity as 21.67GiB**, not 12GB. That is a ~24GB-class card (a 3090/4090-tier GPU), and it still OOM'd on 1,116 4K images. The same report notes the identical dataset trained successfully at 690 4K images on the same hardware/software combination, and that the equivalent job succeeded on an older gsplat version (0.1.11) before regressing on 1.0.0 — meaning this specific OOM is partly a version regression, not solely a scene-size ceiling. [nerfstudio GitHub issue #3214](https://github.com/nerfstudio-project/nerfstudio/issues/3214)

**Correcting for VRAM:** if ~690–1,100 4K images is the rough envelope on a ~21–24GB card, the practical ceiling on a **12GB** card is meaningfully lower — order of a few hundred 4K images at full resolution, not "just over a thousand." No source found gives a precise 12GB-specific number for a large aerial dataset; this is a reasoned scaling-down from the 24GB data point, flagged as such, not a directly measured 12GB figure.

**Real mitigations and what they buy:**
- **`--downscale-factor 2`** (or higher) is the most direct lever — training on half-resolution images roughly quarters the per-image memory footprint, at the direct cost of detail in the trained scene. [nerfstudio issue #3221](https://github.com/nerfstudio-project/nerfstudio/issues/3221)
- **`--pipeline.datamanager.cache-images disk`** moves the image cache off VRAM (and off system RAM) onto disk, trading I/O latency for memory headroom — genuinely useful on a 12GB card, not a placebo.
- **`--pipeline.datamanager.images-on-gpu False`** stops loading the full image set onto the GPU simultaneously.
- **Plain `splatfacto` vs `splatfacto-big`:** documented VRAM envelopes are roughly ~6GB for standard splatfacto on smaller scenes, rising toward ~12GB for the "big" variant — meaning `splatfacto-big` should be assumed to be running near the ceiling on this card, not comfortably inside it. [Radiance Fields: Splatfacto-Big Added to Nerfstudio](https://radiancefields.com/splatfacto-big-added-to-nerfstudio)
- **MCMC strategy vs. default:** MCMC's `cap_max` (default 1,000,000 Gaussians) is a real, usable cap on scene complexity, but MCMC has its own separate memory problem independent of image count — a documented gsplat issue reports MCMC becoming unable to refine models beyond ~18 million points on a **48GB** GPU, because the relocation step needs a contiguous buffer for concatenation on top of the existing point buffer (~4.3GB extra at 18M points). This is a ceiling on Gaussian *count*, not directly on image count, and is a genuinely separate constraint from the "how many source images fit" question. [gsplat GitHub issue #444: MCMC memory consumption](https://github.com/nerfstudio-project/gsplat/issues/444)
- No source found in this pass documents one single "known-good, reproduce-this" configuration that reliably fits a several-hundred-image aerial scene on 12GB end-to-end — the pieces (downscale, disk caching, capped Gaussian count) are each individually documented, but nobody has published the combined recipe with confirmed numbers for an aerial drone dataset at this VRAM tier. **This is a real gap** (see "still unverified" below) and argues for renting a larger card for splat training jobs rather than assuming the 12GB card will cooperate (§B1).

## Part B — Widening the horizon

### B1. Rented GPU compute — the biggest actual gap in prior work

**Pricing, concretely, as of September 2026:**
- **Vast.ai:** RTX 4090 (24GB) median ~$0.37/hr, with listings as low as $0.13–0.14/hr; A100 PCIe ~$0.67/hr median; H100 SXM ~$2.13/hr median. [Multiple aggregator sources, e.g. madebyagents.com GPU rental prices, tech-insider.org RunPod vs Lambda vs Vast.ai 2026]
- **RunPod:** RTX 4090 ~$0.34/hr (Community Cloud) to $0.74/hr (Secure Cloud); H100 $2.79–3.49/hr; **explicitly $0 egress fees**. [RunPod pricing](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)
- **TensorDock:** RTX 4090 24GB from **$0.35–0.37/hr on-demand**, spot as low as $0.20/hr; explicitly "**no ingress/egress fees**"; deploys in ~30 seconds; minimum $5 to start. [TensorDock RTX 4090 pricing page](https://www.tensordock.com/gpu-4090.html)
- **Lambda Labs:** more enterprise-weighted, A100 80GB ~$2.06/hr, H100 SXM ~$2.99/hr — not the cheapest option for this workload, useful mainly as a price ceiling reference. [Multiple 2026 comparison sources]

**Windows instances — a real, useful finding:** RunPod and Vast.ai are both confirmed Linux-only. RunPod's own documentation states plainly: "Windows is not supported: Pods do not currently support Windows" — a structural limitation, since RunPod pods are Linux containers sharing the host kernel. [RunPod docs: Pods overview](https://docs.runpod.io/pods/overview) Vast.ai's own FAQ likewise confirms Linux Docker instances only. **TensorDock is the exception found in this research**: it explicitly offers **Windows 10 support with root access and dedicated GPUs** via KVM virtualization (not containers, so a real Windows OS, not a workaround) — meaning a native Windows RealityScan environment is rentable by the hour at ~$0.35–0.37/hr for a 24GB card, with no egress fees. [TensorDock GPU cloud comparison page]

**Data movement:** on the providers checked that matter for this cost model (RunPod, TensorDock), egress is $0. This removes what would otherwise be a real cost concern for moving several-GB image sets up and orthomosaic/splat outputs back down.

**Costing 8 jobs/month:** a representative job — a few hundred images through ODM (CPU-bound stages dominate wall-clock; a rented GPU instance's accompanying vCPU allocation matters more than the GPU here) plus a splat-training pass — plausibly runs 3–6 hours of rented instance time. At TensorDock/RunPod's ~$0.35–0.37/hr for a 24GB card, that is **roughly $1–3 of compute per job**, or **$8–25/month for 8 jobs** — an order of magnitude cheaper than WebODM Lightning's cheapest paid tier ($24/month, capped at 1,500 images/map) and negligible next to any commercial licence.

**Does this dissolve the shared-host risk, and does it remove the 12GB VRAM ceiling?** Yes to both, directly:
- **Shared-host contention** (RAM fought over with the production stack, VRAM "borrowed" from a pinned embedding model) stops being a hard constraint the moment a heavy job can be shipped to rented, dedicated hardware for a few dollars instead of negotiated for locally. The local box remains right for routine work, inspection, and anything latency-sensitive to the Runner, but it no longer has to be the only place large jobs can run.
- **The 12GB VRAM ceiling on splat training** is removed for any job willing to rent — a 24GB, 48GB, or 80GB card is available on demand at prices that make "just rent a bigger card for this one job" a genuinely reasonable default rather than a heroic option.

This does not change the orthomosaic-engine recommendation (ODM's licence and ecosystem fit are unaffected by where it runs), and it does **not** rescue RealityScan from the §A1 licensing ambiguity — the Windows-rental path was worth checking specifically because it looked like it might, and it turns out the blocker there is legal, not technical.

### B2. Managed / hosted processing (WebODM Lightning and comparable)

- **WebODM Lightning:** cloud-hosted WebODM using a proprietary engine (**LGT**, not the open-source ODX engine self-hosted WebODM/ODM run) — pricing starts at $24/month (Starter, 1,500 images/map cap), $35/month (Pro), up to $99/month (Business, 3,000 images/map cap); a credit-based per-job option exists with 150 free credits for new accounts as of mid-2026. [webodm.net pricing/FAQ pages]
- **Output quality vs. self-hosted:** LGT is explicitly marketed as "designed for highest accuracy outputs, support for arbitrary coordinate systems, higher quality 3D models" versus the open-source ODX engine — i.e., Lightning is not simply "ODM in the cloud," it is a different, proprietary engine with claimed (not independently benchmarked in sources found) quality advantages. Both are described by secondary sources as capable of industry-standard 2–5cm relative accuracy with proper GCP placement and overlap. No independent, numbers-based ODX-vs-LGT comparison was found. [webodm.net FAQ]
- **API-drivable?** Yes — WebODM's open-source API (token auth, project/task creation, multipart image upload, JSON-encoded processing options) is documented and usable for automation; Lightning's own API is advertised at webodm.net/api but I did not find a detailed automation-specific reference beyond the marketing/quickstart pages. [docs.webodm.org quickstart]
- **Does per-job hosted processing beat operating infrastructure for a one-person business?** For pure orthomosaic work at Lightning's image caps, plausibly yes on convenience — no infrastructure, no RAM/disk sizing exercise, month-to-month cost. But it caps out at 3,000 images/map on the most expensive advertised tier, has no bearing on the splat-training half of the business (Lightning doesn't do Gaussian Splatting at all), and costs more per job than the rented-GPU-plus-self-run-ODM path in §B1 once volume is above a trivial level. **Verdict: not a replacement for the pipeline, but a legitimate overflow option for an unusually large single ortho job that would otherwise strain the local host and isn't worth spinning up a rented GPU instance for.**

### B4. Lifting the Mini 5 Pro ceiling without new hardware

- **PPK via NRCan/CORS data:** NRCan's CSRS-PPP service is real, actively maintained (updated 29 January 2026 to IGS Repro3 products), and free. [NRCan CSRS-PPP updates](https://webapp.csrs-scrs.nrcan-rncan.gc.ca/geod/tools-outils/ppp-info.php?locale=en) **But PPK fundamentally requires the drone itself to log raw GNSS carrier-phase observations** (typically as a RINEX file) during flight, which is then corrected against a base station's (or CORS network's) simultaneous raw log. This is a hardware/firmware capability specific to RTK/PPK-equipped drones (DJI's own enterprise line — Phantom 4 RTK, Matrice RTK series — and third-party add-on modules like Emlid's Reach). I found **no evidence the DJI Mini 5 Pro (or the Mini line generally) exposes raw GNSS observation logging**, and every PPK-capable-drone workflow found in this research assumes exactly that capability as a starting point. This is stated as a reasoned technical conclusion, not a directly confirmed negative from DJI documentation — I could not find an explicit DJI statement that the Mini 5 Pro *lacks* this feature, only an absence of any source describing it *having* the feature, which for a well-documented consumer product line over several search attempts is reasonably strong evidence of absence. **Practical conclusion: PPK against NRCan/CORS data is not available to this operator on this airframe, regardless of how good the free Canadian base-station data is** — the gap is the drone, not the post-processing service. [TOPODRONE: DJI Mavic Mini PPK](https://topodrone.com/blog/921) (an aftermarket module bolted onto a *different*, older Mini model, illustrating the point: it took added hardware, not firmware, to get PPK onto a Mini-class airframe at all)
- **Bundle-adjustment techniques that are realistic without RTK:** this is the one genuinely actionable finding in B4, and it is concrete and well-sourced. The dominant source of vertical error on nadir-only, non-RTK drone surveys is the **"doming effect"** — the bundle adjustment misattributes real surface curvature (or an actual dome-shaped systematic bias) to lens distortion because a pure nadir-grid dataset lacks the geometric diversity to separate the two. Per James & Robson (2014), a realistic self-calibrated nadir-only survey domes by roughly **8 inches** (worse in bad cases, up to several feet); adding **oblique imagery at 30–45°** around the site (a genuinely flyable addition for this operator, costing extra flight time, not new hardware) reduces the dome by **up to two orders of magnitude**, down to roughly **1 inch** in the cited study. A second, independent lever — **rolling-shutter correction** — improved vertical RMSE from 1.6ft to 4.2in in Pix4D testing on a separate dataset. A cross-grid (second pass at 90°) helps but is explicitly noted as insufficient alone on flat terrain. [Aerocartwright: Why Your Drone Map Looks Warped — the Doming Effect Explained](https://aerocartwright.com/library/drone-map-doming-effect/) A related peer-reviewed source corroborates the same direction of effect at a different site type: combining nadir with oblique passes brought vertical RMSE from a 20–40cm nadir-only baseline to under 3cm on a GCP-free site using only direct GNSS positioning. [MDPI Drones: Enhancing Direct Georeferencing Using RTK UAVs and SfM for Large-Scale Infrastructure](https://doi.org/10.3390/drones8120736) (note: that specific study used RTK-equipped UAVs for the direct-positioning half, so its absolute numbers don't transfer directly to a consumer-GPS Mini 5 Pro — but the *oblique-fixes-doming* mechanism is the same physics regardless of positioning hardware, and is the actionable takeaway.)
- **Realistic for a solo operator?** Yes, for the oblique-imagery mitigation specifically — it costs extra flight time on an already-scheduled visit and no new hardware or software, and directly targets the exact failure mode ("no RTK means absolute accuracy is bounded") the design document already flags as a known limitation. It will not produce survey-grade absolute accuracy — nothing here manufactures a GNSS fix better than the receiver provides — but it is a genuine, low-cost lever on the *relative*/vertical-shape quality that is well within "visual-grade" scope, and the source explicitly flags the Mini-class consumer GPS/no-GCP combination as "highest consumer risk... not recommended for vertical deliverables without ground control," which is a useful, blunt calibration check against overclaiming what Phase 1 can honestly promise.
